Most insurance advice comes in the form of a checklist: get life insurance, get disability insurance, get an umbrella policy, get general liability if you own a business. Checklists are easy to write and easy to sell, but they don't answer the question that actually matters. How much insurance coverage do you need? That number isn't found in a brochure. It comes from your balance sheet, your cash flow, and the specific exposures tied to your business or household.

At BussCPA, we sit across from automotive shop owners, construction contractors, agriculture producers, and families in and around Hartford, SD who are paying for coverage that doesn't match their actual risk. Some are underinsured on the one exposure that could wipe out a decade of savings. Others are paying premiums on policies that duplicate coverage they already have. Both mistakes come from the same root cause: nobody ran the numbers.

## The real question isn't what insurance to buy, it's how much risk you're carrying

Insurance is a transfer mechanism. You're paying a company to absorb a financial loss you can't or don't want to absorb yourself. That means the right amount of coverage is a function of two things: what you'd lose if the bad event happened, and what you can afford to lose without outside help.

A farmer with $1.2 million in equipment and a fully paid-off combine has a different risk profile than one still financing three years into a loan. A construction contractor bidding $400,000 jobs needs different liability limits than one bidding $40,000 remodels. A family with $3 million in net worth spread across a family trust needs a different personal liability strategy than a young couple renting an apartment in Hartford. None of these differences show up on a generic insurance checklist. They show up on a balance sheet.

## Start with your balance sheet, not an insurance brochure

Before anyone talks about policy types, we look at three numbers: net worth, annual cash flow, and the size of the largest single asset or liability exposure. That third number is the one most people skip.

For a business owner, the largest exposure might be a single piece of equipment, a fleet of vehicles, or a lawsuit from a job site injury. For a household, it might be the equity in a home, an inheritance held in trust, or the income a sole earner brings in every year. Once you know that number, you can size coverage against it instead of guessing.

Here's a simplified version of how that looks across a few client types we work with regularly:

| Client type | Primary exposure | Coverage question to ask |
|---|---|---|
| Automotive shop owner | Fleet vehicles, liability from repairs | Does liability coverage match the value of vehicles typically on the lot? |
| Construction contractor | Job site injury, equipment loss | Do liability limits match the size of your largest active contract? |
| Agriculture operation | Equipment value, seasonal payroll, crop loss | Does workers' comp reflect actual payroll classification codes for seasonal labor? |
| Family with a trust | Net worth held outside liquid accounts | Does umbrella coverage extend to trust-held assets, not just personal ones? |

This is the starting point for a real insurance needs analysis, not a swap-in replacement for one. The specifics of your loan balances, contract sizes, and payroll structure change the math every year.

## Business coverage should match the exposure, not the industry norm

Agriculture clients often carry payroll and tax obligations that look nothing like a retail business or a professional office. Seasonal labor, family employees, and equipment that moves between owned and leased status all change what workers' compensation and property coverage should look like. A blanket policy sized for a generic "farm business" often either overpays for equipment that's already depreciated to near zero or underpays for labor exposure during harvest.

Construction contractors face a similar mismatch. General liability limits are often set once, at the time the policy is written, and never revisited as contract sizes grow. A contractor bidding $150,000 jobs three years ago and $600,000 jobs today, but still carrying the original liability limit, is one job-site incident away from a gap that outstrips the policy. The fix isn't more insurance across the board. It's matching the limit to the current largest contract on the books.

Business insurance premiums are generally deductible as an ordinary business expense, which the [IRS outlines in Publication 535](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p535). That deductibility matters for cash flow planning, but it shouldn't be the reason a policy gets bought or kept. A deduction on a premium you don't need is still money out the door.

## Personal coverage should protect net worth, not fill a checklist

On the personal side, the same logic applies. Life insurance exists to replace income or cover a liability, like a mortgage or a business loan personally guaranteed by an owner. If that liability doesn't exist, or if net worth has grown enough to self-insure it, the policy that made sense at 32 may not make sense at 52.

Families and individuals working with trusts or family offices face a different question: does the coverage extend to assets held outside a personal name? An umbrella policy written years ago, before assets moved into a trust structure, can leave a real gap even though the premium keeps getting paid on time. Attorneys and engineers we work with often carry professional liability coverage that's well matched to their practice, but personal umbrella coverage that hasn't been revisited since before they made partner or took on a larger ownership stake.

## Where over-insuring quietly costs you

Over-insuring rarely looks dramatic. It looks like a $180-a-month rider on a life policy for a mortgage that was paid off four years ago. It looks like duplicate coverage between a personal auto policy and a business fleet policy after a vehicle changed hands. It looks like a disability policy sized against income from a business that's since been sold or restructured.

Each of these, on its own, seems small. Stacked across a decade, they add up to real money that could have gone toward debt payoff, retirement contributions, or reinvestment in the business. We see this pattern often enough among construction and agriculture clients that we now build a coverage review into our advisory conversations, the same way we'd flag a [money mindset](/resources/your-money-mindset-is-costing-your-small-business-more-than-2) issue that's quietly draining a business owner's margins.

## A simple framework for an insurance needs analysis

Running your own version of this doesn't require a spreadsheet built by an actuary. Three questions get you most of the way there:

1. What is my largest single financial exposure right now, in dollars, not categories?
2. What could I absorb out of pocket without touching savings, retirement accounts, or the business's operating cash?
3. Has anything changed in the last 12 months, a new contract size, a paid-off loan, a trust formation, that makes an old policy oversized or undersized?

If the answer to that third question is yes, the policy is due for a review regardless of when it's set to renew.

## Common Questions

**How much insurance coverage do you actually need?**
Enough to cover your largest quantifiable exposure minus what you can reasonably absorb out of pocket. That number comes from your balance sheet and cash flow, not a standard percentage of income or a generic industry checklist.

**What types of insurance matter most for financial protection?**
For most households, that's life insurance tied to an actual income replacement need, disability coverage, and umbrella liability sized to net worth. For businesses, it's general liability, property, and workers' compensation sized to current contract volume or payroll, not the levels set when the policy was first written.

**How often should a business insurance coverage checklist be reviewed?**
At least annually, and immediately after any change in contract size, payroll, equipment value, or business structure. A construction contractor whose average job size doubles in a year needs a liability review well before the next scheduled renewal.

**Can over-insuring actually hurt a business?**
Yes, in cash flow terms. Premiums on duplicate or oversized coverage reduce the cash available for debt paydown, equipment purchases, or reinvestment, without adding any real protection once the exposure has shrunk or disappeared.
