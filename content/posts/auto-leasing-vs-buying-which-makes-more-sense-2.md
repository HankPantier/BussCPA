A client pulled into our Hartford office last month with a decision he'd been putting off for six weeks: lease a new F-150 for his construction crew, or buy one outright and take the deduction. His finance guy at the dealership had a pitch. His gut had an opinion. Neither of those is a tax strategy.

Leasing versus buying isn't a personality question. It's a math question that depends on how much you drive, whether the vehicle is titled to a business, and what your cash flow looks like over the next three to five years. The dealership wants to close a deal today. We want you to make the call that actually saves you money once the tax return is filed.

## Start with how the vehicle will actually be used

If this is a personal car for commuting and weekend errands, the tax code mostly doesn't care whether you lease or buy. Your decision comes down to cash flow, how long you keep vehicles, and how many miles you rack up. Lease terms typically cap you at 10,000 to 15,000 miles a year, and every mile over that costs 15 to 30 cents at lease-end. If you're driving from Hartford to Sioux Falls for work several times a week, that cap gets expensive fast.

If the vehicle is titled to a business or used for self-employment, the analysis changes completely. Now depreciation, Section 179, and business-use percentage all come into play, and this is where a generic auto blog stops being useful and a CPA conversation starts.

## The tax angle dealerships don't explain well

When you buy a business vehicle, you may be able to deduct a large chunk of the purchase price in the year you place it in service, under Section 179 of the tax code. The [IRS Publication 946 guidance on depreciating property](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p946) lays out the rules, including annual dollar limits and the vehicle weight thresholds that determine how much you can deduct upfront versus over time. Heavy pickups and vans used for construction or agriculture often qualify for larger first-year deductions than a passenger sedan, which matters if you're outfitting a fleet.

Lease payments work differently. You generally deduct the business-use percentage of your monthly payment as an ordinary expense, spread evenly across the lease term. There's no big first-year write-off, but there's also no depreciation recapture to worry about when you turn the vehicle back in. For a business with unpredictable income, spreading the deduction evenly can actually be easier to plan around than a lump sum.

Here's the trade-off in plain terms:

| Factor | Buying | Leasing |
|---|---|---|
| Upfront tax deduction | Can be large (Section 179, bonus depreciation) | Spread evenly over lease term |
| Monthly payment | Usually higher | Usually lower |
| Mileage limits | None | Typically 10,000–15,000/year |
| Ownership at end | Yes, and you can sell or trade | No, unless you buy out the lease |
| Best for | High-mileage drivers, businesses wanting a big current-year deduction | Lower-mileage drivers, businesses that want predictable payments |

## Why cash flow matters more than the sticker price

A lot of buyers focus on the monthly payment and stop there. That's the dealership's favorite number to talk about, because it's usually the smallest one on the page. What matters more is what that payment does to your working capital over the life of the vehicle.

Interest rates on auto loans have moved around significantly over the past few years. The Federal Reserve's [G.19 Consumer Credit report](https://www.federalreserve.gov/releases/g19/current/) tracks these trends, and the rate environment when you sign matters just as much as the price of the vehicle. A construction company financing three trucks at once feels a rate difference far more than someone buying a single commuter car.

For agriculture operations, this gets even more specific. Farm vehicles often serve double duty, hauling equipment one week and running personal errands the next, which means the business-use percentage has to be tracked carefully to support any deduction claimed. We work through this exact calculation with our agriculture clients regularly, because their payroll and tax situations already look different from a typical retail or professional services business, and vehicle decisions are no exception.

## A simple framework before you sign anything

Run through these four questions before you talk to a dealer:

1. **Will this vehicle be used more than 50% for business?** If yes, Section 179 and depreciation become real tools. If no, skip straight to comparing loan versus lease payments on personal cash flow terms.
2. **How many miles do you put on a vehicle each year?** Over 15,000 consistently, buying usually wins. Under that, leasing starts to look attractive, especially if you like driving a newer vehicle every three years.
3. **Do you want a large deduction this year, or steady deductions over several years?** A profitable year might call for the upfront Section 179 hit. A leaner year might benefit more from spreading the expense out.
4. **What does your balance sheet look like in three years?** Buying builds equity you can trade or sell. Leasing keeps your balance sheet lighter but leaves you with nothing to show at the end of the term.

## Common Questions

**Is it better to lease or buy a car for tax purposes?**
It depends on business use. If the vehicle is used more than 50% for business, buying often allows a larger upfront deduction through Section 179, while leasing spreads a smaller deduction evenly across the lease term. For personal vehicles, tax treatment plays almost no role in the decision.

**Can I write off a leased vehicle on my taxes?**
Yes, if the vehicle is used for business, you can typically deduct the business-use percentage of your lease payments as an ordinary expense. You'll need mileage logs or another method to support the business-use percentage if you're ever asked to document it.

**What is the Section 179 vehicle deduction limit?**
Section 179 limits vary by vehicle weight and type, with heavier trucks and vans generally qualifying for larger deductions than passenger cars. The [IRS Publication 946](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p946) outlines current thresholds and how bonus depreciation interacts with the Section 179 limit.

**Does leasing or buying make more sense for a small construction business?**
Construction businesses with high annual mileage and a need for heavier trucks often lean toward buying, since Section 179 deductions on qualifying vehicles can be substantial. Businesses with lighter, more predictable vehicle needs sometimes prefer leasing for the steadier monthly cash flow.
