A portfolio built five years ago and never touched again isn't a strategy. It's a guess that happened to work out, or a mistake waiting to surface on your next tax return. Markets move every day, but the discipline of checking whether your investments still match your goals doesn't happen automatically. That gap, between what you set up once and what your money actually does over time, is where returns quietly leak out.

At BussCPA, we talk with business owners across Hartford, South Dakota who assume their 401(k) or brokerage account is on autopilot because they picked a target-date fund or a model portfolio when they opened the account. Then a construction company sells for a strong multiple, a farm has a windfall year, or a bonus hits, and the "balanced" portfolio from three years ago is nowhere close to balanced anymore.

## Why "set it and forget it" quietly costs you money

Stock and bond markets don't grow at the same rate. Say you start with a 70/30 mix of stocks and bonds in a taxable brokerage account. If equities outperform bonds for several years running, that same account can drift to 82/18 or higher without a single trade. You didn't decide to take on more risk. The market decided for you.

That drift matters more than it sounds. A portfolio that's 12 percentage points more aggressive than intended carries meaningfully more downside exposure in a correction, and most investors don't notice until a bad quarter forces the conversation. The Federal Reserve's household finance research has tracked how unmanaged risk exposure and infrequent portfolio review correlate with larger losses for individual investors during downturns. [Federal Reserve Notes on Household Finance and Investment Behavior](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/notes/feds-notes/default.htm)

## Asset allocation strategy starts with an honest risk tolerance conversation

Before any rebalancing conversation makes sense, the allocation itself has to reflect where you actually are, not where you were when you opened the account. A 34-year-old engineer with 30 years until retirement can absorb a market drop differently than a 61-year-old attorney two years from winding down a practice. Risk tolerance is a function of timeline, income stability, and what else sits on your balance sheet, not a personality trait.

| Investor profile | Typical time horizon | Allocation tilt |
|---|---|---|
| Early-career professional | 25+ years | Growth-heavy, 80-90% equities |
| Mid-career business owner | 10-20 years | Balanced, 60-70% equities |
| Pre-retirement or retired | 0-10 years | Income-focused, 30-50% equities |
| Family trust or multi-generational account | Indefinite | Custom, driven by beneficiary needs |

Family offices and trusts we work with often need a different framework entirely, because the account isn't funding one person's retirement. It's funding multiple beneficiaries across decades, which changes both the risk tolerance and the tax planning behind every rebalancing decision.

## Rebalancing your portfolio takes a repeatable schedule

Rebalancing means selling what's grown past its target weight and buying what's fallen below it, on a schedule you actually follow. Annually is common. Some households rebalance whenever an asset class drifts more than 5 percentage points from target, whichever comes first.

The hard part isn't the math. It's the behavior. Rebalancing forces you to sell some of what's been winning and buy more of what's been lagging, which feels wrong in the moment and is exactly the point. Diversification works because asset classes take turns leading and lagging, not because everything moves together.

## Tax-efficient rebalancing is where DIY investors get expensive surprises

Here's where a CPA's involvement changes the outcome, not just the process. Selling appreciated stock in a taxable account triggers capital gains. Rebalance carelessly every year in a brokerage account and you can generate a tax bill that erodes the gains you were trying to lock in.

A few moves make a real difference:

- Rebalancing inside tax-deferred accounts (401(k)s, IRAs) first, where trades don't trigger current-year tax
- Directing new contributions toward underweight asset classes instead of selling winners, when possible
- Harvesting losses in a down year to offset gains elsewhere in the same portfolio
- Timing larger rebalancing trades around other income events, like a year with lower business income

South Dakota residents get one advantage in this conversation: no state income tax on capital gains, so the entire tax-efficiency conversation happens at the federal level. That's still real money. Long-term capital gains rates run from 0% to 20% depending on income, plus a possible 3.8% net investment income tax above certain thresholds. A CPA who can see your full tax picture, including business income, farm income, or trust distributions, catches opportunities a brokerage's default rebalancing tool never will.

## Concentration risk looks different for farmers, contractors, and business owners

Most personal finance advice assumes your wealth sits in a diversified brokerage account. For construction and agriculture clients we work with in Hartford, that's rarely true. A construction company owner might have 60% of net worth tied up in the business itself, plus equipment and real estate. An agriculture client's balance sheet often runs the other direction: land, grain inventory, and machinery that don't move like stocks and bonds, with payroll and tax timing that follows the growing season rather than a calendar quarter.

That changes what "balanced" means. If your operating business already represents significant risk concentration, the investment portfolio sitting outside it should probably lean more conservative than a generic model portfolio would suggest, even if your age and income would otherwise point toward an aggressive allocation. This comes up directly with construction clients weighing how a [cost segregation study](/resources/cost-segregation-studies-are-a-construction-companys-most-ov-2) on a new facility affects both near-term cash flow and how aggressively they should invest outside the business that same year.

## Common Questions

**How often should I rebalance my investment portfolio?**
Once a year is a reasonable default for most households, or whenever an asset class drifts more than 5 percentage points from its target weight. Business owners with variable income should time larger rebalancing trades around years when their business income is lower, to reduce the combined tax hit.

**What's the difference between asset allocation and diversification?**
Asset allocation is the split between broad categories: stocks, bonds, cash, real estate. Diversification is spreading risk within each category, across sectors, company sizes, and geographies, so a downturn in one area doesn't sink the whole portfolio.

**Does rebalancing always trigger a tax bill?**
Not if it happens inside a 401(k) or IRA, where trades aren't taxed in the year they occur. In taxable brokerage accounts, rebalancing can trigger capital gains, which is why directing new contributions toward underweight assets and harvesting losses in the same year both help reduce the tax cost.

**How does owning a farm or construction business change my investment strategy?**
If a large share of your net worth already sits in an operating business, equipment, or land, that concentration counts as risk exposure even though it isn't a stock. Your outside investment portfolio often needs to run more conservative than your age alone would suggest, to offset the concentration you're already carrying.
