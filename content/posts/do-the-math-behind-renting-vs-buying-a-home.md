Ask five people whether you should buy or rent, and you'll get five answers rooted in feelings: pride of ownership, fear of "throwing money away" on rent, or a bad experience with a leaky basement. None of that belongs in the decision. Buying or renting a home is a financial statement question, and it deserves the same discipline you'd apply to any five- or ten-year commitment on your balance sheet. At BussCPA, we work with individuals and households across Hartford and the surrounding area who are trying to figure out exactly this, and the answer almost never comes from a gut feeling. It comes from a spreadsheet.

## Put the decision on paper before you put in an offer

A mortgage payment and a rent check both leave your checking account, but they don't do the same thing to your net worth. Rent is a full expense, gone the moment it clears. A mortgage payment splits into interest, which is an expense, and principal, which builds equity, an asset on your personal balance sheet. That distinction is why the comparison isn't rent-versus-mortgage-payment. It's total cost of occupancy versus total cost of ownership, measured over the years you actually expect to stay put.

Here's a simplified version of that comparison for a $280,000 home in the Hartford area, financed with a 20% down payment ($56,000) on a 30-year fixed mortgage at 6.5%, versus renting a comparable house at $1,650 a month:

| Cost category | Buying (Year 1) | Renting (Year 1) |
|---|---|---|
| Monthly payment / rent | $1,415 (P&I) | $1,650 |
| Property taxes (est.) | $280/mo | $0 |
| Insurance | $95/mo | $20/mo (renters) |
| Maintenance (1% of value/yr) | $233/mo | $0 |
| Principal paid (builds equity) | ~$310/mo | $0 |
| Net cash cost | ~$1,713/mo | ~$1,670/mo |

On a pure cash-out basis, renting looks slightly cheaper here. But $310 a month in principal isn't a cost, it's forced savings. Run that same household through year five, once rates on new leases have climbed and the mortgage payment hasn't moved, and the math tilts the other direction. That's the exercise worth doing before signing anything: [model your specific numbers for years one, five, and ten](/resources/do-the-math-behind-renting-vs-buying-a-home-2), not just the move-in month.

## The tax deduction is smaller than most people assume

A lot of buyers walk into a purchase assuming the mortgage interest deduction will bail out an otherwise thin margin. For most households, it won't move the needle the way it used to. Under current rules, you can deduct interest on acquisition debt up to $750,000, but only if you itemize, and the standard deduction is high enough now that most taxpayers don't. The IRS lays out the specifics in [Topic No. 505, Interest Expense](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc505), and it's worth reading before you count on this deduction as part of your affordability math. If you're already itemizing because of business income, charitable giving, or other deductions, the mortgage interest deduction adds real value. If you're a standard-deduction household, treat it as a rounding error, not a reason to buy.

## What renting keeps liquid that buying locks up

The down payment conversation usually stops at "can I afford it," but the better question is "what else could that money do." $56,000 sitting in home equity isn't earning a return you can access without a sale or a home equity loan, and it isn't diversified. The same $56,000 invested in a balanced portfolio, split appropriately between equities and fixed income for your age and risk tolerance, keeps working and stays liquid. According to the Federal Reserve's [Survey of Consumer Finances](https://www.federalreserve.gov/publications/files/scf20.pdf), home equity remains the largest single asset for most U.S. households, which is exactly why concentration risk deserves a second look before you commit six figures to one property in one town. We've written about how to keep a portfolio genuinely balanced rather than set-and-forget in our piece on [strategically balancing your investment portfolio](/resources/strategically-balancing-your-investment-portfolio-why-set-it-2), and the same logic applies here: don't let one asset, even your home, dominate your net worth without a plan.

Renting also keeps you flexible. If a job offer comes through in Sioux Falls, or your household size changes, breaking a lease costs a fraction of what selling a house costs in commissions, repairs, and closing costs, typically 8-10% of the sale price when everything is added up. That flexibility has a dollar value, even if it never shows up on a bank statement.

## Households with irregular income need a different formula

We work with a fair number of agriculture families and construction contractors in the Hartford area, and for them, the buy-versus-rent math has an extra variable: income timing. A row crop operation might see 70% of its cash come in during a two-month window after harvest. A framing contractor might have a strong Q3 and a slow Q1. Qualifying for a mortgage, and comfortably carrying one, means underwriting the payment against your worst month, not your average month. A household with a steady $75,000 salary and a household with $75,000 in average but lumpy farm or contracting income can qualify for the identical mortgage on paper and face completely different real-world risk.

This is where a fixed monthly rent payment sometimes makes more sense during a volatile stretch of a business, even if the long-term ownership math looks better. We help agriculture and construction clients build a 12-month cash flow projection before they commit to either a mortgage or a long-term lease, because a house payment that works in July and fails in February isn't a good decision no matter what the equity math says.

## Build the actual numbers before you decide

The honest version of this decision requires four inputs specific to you: how long you plan to stay, your marginal tax rate and whether you itemize, what a down payment would otherwise earn if invested, and how stable your income is month to month. Run those four inputs through a real projection, not a five-minute online calculator that assumes a national average rent increase and ignores your actual tax situation. We build these projections for individual and household clients as part of our advisory work, using the same real-time financial visibility we give our business clients through BASE, our outsourced accounting service. If you already work with us on personal tax prep, we can pull your actual marginal rate and itemization status straight from your return instead of guessing.

If cash flow discipline is the real issue behind the buy-or-rent question, it's worth checking for smaller leaks first. Recurring subscriptions and small monthly commitments add up the same way a mortgage payment does, just quieter. Our post on [subscription creep](/resources/take-back-control-of-subscription-creep) is a good starting point if you suspect your budget has more flexibility than your bank statement suggests.

## Common Questions

**Is it always cheaper to rent than to buy?**
No. Renting usually has a lower month-one cash cost, but buying builds equity through principal payments that renting doesn't. Whether buying wins depends on how long you stay in the home, your local property tax rate, and what your down payment would otherwise earn if invested. A five-year time horizon changes the answer compared to a fifteen-year one.

**Does the mortgage interest deduction make buying automatically better?**
Only if you itemize deductions, and most households now take the standard deduction instead. Check [IRS Topic No. 505](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc505) for the current rules on deducting mortgage interest before you factor this into your affordability calculation.

**How much does income volatility matter for agriculture or construction households?**
It matters more than the interest rate in many cases. A mortgage payment needs to survive your slowest month, not your average month. We build 12-month cash flow projections for farm and contracting clients specifically because a payment that's comfortable in a strong season can strain a household during a lean one.

**What's a reasonable rule of thumb for how long I need to stay to make buying worthwhile?**
Most households need to stay three to five years just to recoup closing costs and selling commissions, which typically run 8-10% of the sale price. Beyond that window, equity built through principal payments and any home price appreciation starts to outweigh the upfront transaction costs, but the exact break-even point depends on your down payment size and local market conditions.
