A cabin on Lake Madison or a place near the Black Hills isn't just a lifestyle purchase. It's a tax decision, and the IRS has specific rules about how many days you use it, how many days you rent it, and what that mix means for your return. Get the mix right and a second home can shelter income and generate deductions. Get it wrong and you could owe tax on rental income you thought was tax-free, or lose deductions you assumed you'd get.

Most of what you'll find online about second homes is realtor advice: location, resale value, financing tips. That's useful for the purchase decision, but it skips the part that determines your actual after-tax return. This is where a CPA earns their fee, not just at filing time but before you sign the purchase agreement.

## The 14 day rule most buyers have never heard of

The IRS draws a hard line based on personal use versus rental days, and understanding it changes how you should plan the year.

If you rent the property for 14 days or fewer during the year, the rental income is not taxable at all, regardless of how much you charge. You don't even have to report it. That's the rule people mean when they mention the "14 day rule." Rent your lake house for a wedding weekend or a hunting season stretch and pocket the cash tax-free, as long as you stay under that 14-day threshold.

Cross that line and rent it for 15 days or more, and the property gets classified based on personal use. If your personal use exceeds the greater of 14 days or 10% of the days it's rented at fair market value, the IRS treats it as a personal residence with limited rental deductions. If personal use falls below that threshold, it's treated as a rental property, and a very different set of rules applies, including the ability to deduct losses against other income in some cases. The full mechanics are laid out in [IRS Publication 527](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p527) and summarized in [IRS Tax Topic 415](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc415).

## Second home vs investment property

People use these terms interchangeably, but the IRS doesn't, and the distinction changes your deduction limits, your mortgage interest treatment, and even your loan terms.

A second home is a property you use personally for a meaningful chunk of the year, with limited rental activity layered on top. Mortgage interest and property taxes follow the same rules as your primary residence, subject to the usual limits, and any rental income you do earn is capped by the personal-use test above.

An investment property is one where personal use is minimal or nonexistent. Once a property crosses into investment territory, you're generally filing Schedule E, depreciating the building, and potentially deducting losses against other income if you materially participate or qualify under the passive activity rules. Lenders also treat these differently: investment property mortgages typically carry higher rates and larger down payment requirements than second-home loans, because banks price in the risk that you'll walk away from a rental before you'll walk away from a place your family actually uses.

The strategic point: the same physical property can be structured as either one, depending on how you manage your calendar. A family that limits personal use to three weeks a year and rents the rest at fair market rates is building an investment property with real depreciation benefits. A family that uses the cabin all summer and rents it out for a few fall weekends is running a second home with tax-free rental income under the 14-day rule. Neither is wrong. They're just different plans, and the plan should be set before the year starts, not reconstructed from memory in April.

## What renting out a second home does to your taxes

Once you're renting past the 14-day threshold, the accounting gets more involved, and this is where a lot of second-home owners lose money simply from not tracking things correctly.

You'll need to allocate expenses, mortgage interest, property taxes, utilities, repairs, and depreciation, between personal and rental use based on the number of days each applies. A property rented 90 days and used personally 30 days allocates expenses on a 90/120 basis for the rental portion. Miss this allocation and you either overstate your rental deductions (an audit flag) or understate them (money left on the table).

Depreciation is the piece owners forget most often. Even a property with modest rental income can generate a meaningful depreciation deduction, and if the numbers work out right, that deduction can offset other rental income or, in limited cases, a portion of your ordinary income. This is exactly the kind of math where a cost segregation study, more common in commercial real estate but occasionally relevant here, can accelerate the benefit. We've written about how this works for larger properties in our piece on [cost segregation studies](/resources/cost-segregation-studies-are-a-construction-companys-most-ov-2), and the same underlying depreciation logic applies at a smaller scale to vacation rental income.

## A quick reference for the thresholds

| Situation | Rental days | Personal use | Tax treatment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Occasional rental | 14 or fewer | Any amount | Rental income not taxable, not reported |
| Second home with light rental | 15+ | More than 14 days or 10% of rental days | Limited rental deductions, treated as personal residence |
| Investment property | 15+ | 14 days or less, or under 10% of rental days | Full Schedule E treatment, depreciation, possible loss deductions |

These thresholds sound simple on paper. In practice, tracking days correctly, especially when family members use the property, or when you're doing maintenance and repairs on-site, is where owners get tripped up. A day spent primarily on repairs and maintenance doesn't count as personal use, even if you sleep there. That distinction alone has changed the classification for several property owners we've worked with.

## Common Questions

**How many days can I rent my second home without paying tax on the income?**
Up to 14 days per year. Rent it for 14 days or fewer and the income is not taxable and doesn't need to be reported, regardless of the rental rate you charge, under the rule described in [IRS Tax Topic 415](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc415).

**What's the real difference between a second home and an investment property for tax purposes?**
A second home involves substantial personal use, generally more than 14 days a year or 10% of rental days, and gets limited rental deductions. An investment property has minimal personal use and qualifies for full rental deductions, including depreciation, under [IRS Publication 527](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p527).

**Do I have to report rental income if I only rent my cabin out a few weekends a year?**
If total rental days are 14 or fewer, no. If you exceed that, all rental income becomes reportable, and you'll need to track expenses and personal-use days to calculate your deductions correctly.

**Can I deduct losses from renting out my second home?**
Only if the property qualifies as a rental (personal use under the threshold) rather than a personal residence with incidental rental. Even then, passive activity loss rules may limit how much you can deduct against other income in a given year.
