A builder pouring foundations in Hartford this spring is running a different tax calculus than the one they used in 2023. The One Big Beautiful Bill Act reshaped who qualifies for the home construction exception under IRC Section 460, and the practical effect is straightforward: a lot more residential contractors, developers, and spec builders can now skip percentage-of-completion accounting altogether. That is not a footnote. It changes when you recognize income, how much tax you pay in a given year, and whether your cash flow lines up with your tax bill.

If your firm has been stuck reporting revenue on jobs that are 40% complete but haven't collected a dime past the deposit, this is worth twenty minutes of your accountant's time before your 2026 contracts get signed.

## The percentage-of-completion trap most builders knew too well

Under the old rules, long-term construction contracts generally had to use the percentage-of-completion method (PCM) unless a contractor met a small-business gross receipts test, which sat around $30 million in average annual receipts before OBBBA. Miss that threshold, and PCM was mandatory for contracts expected to run longer than two years, home construction or not.

PCM sounds fair in theory. You recognize income as the job progresses, matched to costs incurred. In practice, it front-loads taxable income before cash arrives. A builder finishing a $1.2 million custom home over 14 months might owe tax on income recognized at the 60% completion mark, while the buyer's draw schedule and final closing don't release that cash until months later. For a mid-size residential builder jugging five or six spec homes at once, that mismatch compounds fast and eats into working capital right when material and labor costs need to be paid.

## OBBBA raises the small contractor threshold to $80 million

OBBBA lifted the gross receipts ceiling for the small contractor exception to $80 million, a jump that pulls a substantial number of regional homebuilders and residential developers out of mandatory PCM. Combine that with the existing home construction contract definition under Section 460(e), covering contracts where 80% or more of estimated costs relate to dwelling units or improvements to real property directly related to such units, and the exception now covers builders who previously had no path around percentage-of-completion.

The practical upshot: qualifying home construction contracts can use the completed contract method, cash method, or another permissible method instead of PCM, deferring income recognition until the home is substantially complete and, in many cases, sold. For a builder running $40 to $70 million in annual receipts, that used to be out of reach. Now it isn't.

## Why the 2026 contract date matters more than you think

The expanded exception applies to contracts entered into after the law's enactment date, which for calendar-year taxpayers means every contract signed starting in 2026 falls under the new rules. Contracts already in progress under the old thresholds generally stay locked into whatever method was elected when they were signed. That creates a narrow but real planning window right now, heading into the next contracting season.

Builders who sign a wave of new contracts in early 2026 without revisiting their accounting method could end up running two parallel systems: legacy contracts still on PCM, new contracts eligible for completed contract or cash method. That's manageable with the right bookkeeping structure, but it needs to be set up on purpose, not discovered in March 2027 when the tax return is due.

## Completed contract vs. percentage-of-completion side by side

| Factor | Percentage-of-Completion | Completed Contract (under expanded exception) |
|---|---|---|
| Income recognition | As work progresses, based on costs incurred vs. estimated total costs | Deferred until the home is substantially complete |
| Cash flow alignment | Often mismatched, tax due before cash collected | Tax follows closer to when cash is actually received |
| Administrative burden | Requires ongoing cost-to-complete estimates and look-back calculations | Simpler tracking during construction, one recognition event at completion |
| Look-back method exposure | Applies; may require IRS Form 8697 adjustments | Generally not applicable |
| Best fit | Long multi-year contracts, larger public or commercial builders | Residential builders and developers under the new $80 million threshold |

The look-back method deserves a specific mention. Contractors using PCM sometimes owe additional interest to the IRS when actual costs deviate from estimates, calculated on [IRS Form 8697](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i8697). Moving eligible contracts to completed contract accounting removes that calculation entirely for those jobs, which is one less compliance headache during an already busy build season.

## Making a proactive method change before contracts lock you in

Switching accounting methods isn't automatic just because you now qualify. It typically requires filing Form 3115, Application for Change in Accounting Method, and in most cases this falls under automatic consent procedures, but the timing and the section 481(a) adjustment calculation still need to be handled correctly. Get it wrong and you risk an unfavorable adjustment period or a mismatch between book and tax reporting that follows you for years.

Here's the sequence that actually works for builders we've walked through this with:

1. Pull average annual gross receipts for the prior three years to confirm you fall under the new $80 million threshold.
2. Review contracts currently in progress to identify which ones were signed before enactment and remain locked into PCM.
3. Model the Section 481(a) adjustment for switching existing eligible contracts, and separately plan for new contracts going forward.
4. File Form 3115 well ahead of your 2026 tax year close, not after contracts are already signed and jobs are underway.
5. Update your job costing software and chart of accounts so completed contract tracking doesn't get tangled with legacy PCM jobs still on the books.

Step three is where most of the value gets left on the table. Builders who assume the new method only applies going forward sometimes miss that certain existing contracts, if not yet substantially complete, may also be eligible for a favorable switch. The AICPA's [tax resource library](https://www.aicpa-cima.com/topic/tax) is a useful starting point for the technical mechanics, but the actual numbers need to run against your specific job schedule.

## Common Questions

**What is the home construction exception under IRC 460?**
It's a carve-out from mandatory percentage-of-completion accounting for contracts where at least 80% of estimated costs relate to dwelling units or real property improvements directly tied to those units. Contracts meeting this definition can use methods like completed contract or cash basis instead of PCM.

**Does the OBBBA threshold change apply to contracts already signed?**
Generally no. The expanded $80 million gross receipts threshold applies to contracts entered into after the law's enactment date, meaning calendar-year taxpayers see it apply starting with 2026 contracts. Existing contracts typically stay under the method elected when they were originally signed, though some in-progress jobs may still qualify for review.

**Do I need to file anything to switch accounting methods?**
Yes. A change from percentage-of-completion to completed contract or another method requires Form 3115 and a properly calculated Section 481(a) adjustment. Most builders qualify for automatic consent procedures, but the timing needs to be handled before the tax year closes, not after.

**How does this affect the look-back method and Form 8697?**
Contracts that move off percentage-of-completion generally stop generating look-back interest calculations, since that requirement is tied to PCM. Contracts remaining on PCM still need the look-back calculation detailed in the [IRS instructions for Form 8697](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i8697).
