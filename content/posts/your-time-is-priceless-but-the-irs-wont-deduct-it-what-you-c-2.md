A volunteer firefighter in Hartford who drives to three calls a week, buys his own boots, and spends Saturday mornings at pancake breakfast fundraisers has given the department a genuine amount of money. None of that shows up as a paycheck, so most people assume none of it shows up on a tax return either. That assumption is only half right, and the half that's wrong costs taxpayers real deductions every year.

The IRS draws a sharp line between the value of your time and the money you actually spend to volunteer. Your hours are never deductible, no matter how skilled you are or what a similar service would cost if you billed for it. But the out-of-pocket costs tied to that volunteering, mileage, supplies, uniforms, even certain travel expenses, are often fully deductible if you itemize and keep the right paperwork. Understanding where that line falls matters most during tax season and year-end giving campaigns, when confusion about charitable rules peaks.

## Your hours don't count, but your dollars often do

The rule sounds counterintuitive until you think about how the IRS treats donations generally. A cash gift to a qualified charity is deductible because money left your pocket and went to the organization. A CPA who volunteers 20 hours preparing a nonprofit's books has given something valuable, but nothing left her pocket. [IRS Publication 526](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p526) is explicit on this point: the value of your time or services is never deductible, regardless of your profession or what you'd normally charge.

What changes the math is spending your own money to make that volunteer work happen. Gas to drive to the food shelf. A uniform required by the volunteer fire department. Postage for a fundraising mailing you paid for yourself. Those are unreimbursed volunteer expenses, and they follow the same rules as a cash donation: deductible if the organization qualifies under IRS rules and you keep records to prove it.

## Charitable mileage adds up faster than most volunteers realize

Mileage is the deduction most Hartford-area volunteers leave on the table, largely because they've never tracked it. The charitable mileage rate is 14 cents per mile, a flat rate set by statute that hasn't moved in years, unlike the business mileage rate, which the IRS adjusts annually. You can also deduct actual out-of-pocket costs for gas and oil, though not depreciation, insurance, or general maintenance, if that method produces a larger number and you keep receipts to back it up.

For someone driving to 4-H events, hauling supplies for a church mission trip, or making regular runs for a county extension board, that adds up. A volunteer who logs 1,500 miles a year running errands for a nonprofit thrift store picks up a $210 deduction using the standard rate alone. Parking fees and tolls paid while volunteering are deductible in addition to the mileage rate.

## Supplies, uniforms, and travel costs for nonprofit work

Beyond mileage, a wide range of direct costs qualify as unreimbursed volunteer expenses, as long as the organization is a qualified charity and the spending is directly connected to the volunteer work rather than a general convenience.

| Expense | Deductible? | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Mileage driving to volunteer activities | Yes | 14 cents per mile, or actual gas and oil costs |
| Required uniforms (not usable as everyday clothing) | Yes | Volunteer firefighter gear, scout leader uniforms |
| Supplies purchased for the organization | Yes | Paint for a Habitat build, ingredients for a shelter meal |
| Travel expenses for an overnight volunteer trip | Yes, with limits | No significant element of personal vacation allowed |
| Long-distance phone calls made for the charity | Yes | Must be directly tied to the volunteer role |
| Value of your time or professional services | No | True regardless of your hourly rate |
| Meals eaten during a local volunteer shift | No | Different from meals while traveling overnight |
| Child care so you can volunteer | No | Not connected closely enough to the organization |

Overnight travel is where the rules get more particular. If you travel out of town for a volunteer assignment, say a mission trip or a national conference for a nonprofit board you sit on, you can deduct airfare, lodging, and meals, but only if there's no significant element of personal vacation in the trip. A week in Rapid City that includes two hours of volunteer work and five days of sightseeing won't pass that test.

## Costs that never qualify, no matter how good the cause

A few categories trip people up because they feel like they should count. They don't, and the IRS has been consistent about it.

Child care so a parent can volunteer isn't deductible, even though the volunteering itself is unpaid and worthwhile. Meals you eat during a local volunteer shift aren't deductible either, since that's a personal expense you'd have regardless. And the value of donated services, whether it's a contractor donating labor for a Habitat build or an attorney doing pro bono work for a nonprofit board, is never deductible. Materials that contractor buys and donates are deductible; the hours spent installing them are not.

## Board members and committee volunteers carry extra documentation weight

Family offices, trusts, and closely held businesses in construction and agriculture often have someone sitting on a nonprofit board, whether it's a grain co-op scholarship fund or a construction trade association. Board service creates its own wrinkle: expenses tied to attending meetings, mileage to board functions, and travel for board retreats follow the same unreimbursed expense rules as any other volunteer work, but board members are more likely to hit the $250 threshold that changes what the IRS wants to see.

Once unreimbursed expenses for any single contribution or a related set of expenses reach $250, a canceled check or bank statement isn't enough. You need a contemporaneous written acknowledgment from the organization describing the volunteer services and confirming you didn't receive anything in return. Board members who fly to an annual conference and rack up a $600 travel bill need that letter before they file, not after.

## Records the IRS expects before you claim a deduction

Good recordkeeping is what separates a deduction that survives an inquiry from one that doesn't. For mileage, that means a log with dates, destinations, and purpose, not a year-end estimate. For supplies and uniforms, keep receipts and note which organization they supported. For any unreimbursed expense of $250 or more, get the written acknowledgment before you file, since the IRS won't accept one obtained after the fact if you're audited.

The organization also has to qualify. Not every nonprofit-sounding group qualifies for charitable deductions, and giving to an individual, a political campaign, or a foreign organization without proper affiliation won't count even if the cause is compelling. The [IRS Charities & Nonprofits Resource Hub](https://www.irs.gov/charities-non-profits) lets you confirm an organization's status before you assume your expenses qualify.

One more detail catches people off guard: none of this matters if you take the standard deduction. Volunteer expenses, like other charitable contributions, only reduce your tax bill if you itemize on Schedule A. For a lot of households, that means running the numbers both ways before assuming a year of volunteer mileage and supplies will move the needle.

## Common Questions

**Can I deduct the value of my time if I volunteer as a professional, like a CPA or attorney?**
No. The IRS treats professional services donated to a charity the same as any other volunteer time: not deductible, regardless of your normal hourly rate or the market value of the work.

**What mileage rate applies to charitable driving in 2024 and 2025?**
The charitable mileage rate is 14 cents per mile, set by statute rather than adjusted annually like the business mileage rate. You can also deduct actual gas and oil costs instead, if you keep receipts and that method produces a larger deduction.

**Do I need a receipt for every volunteer expense I want to deduct?**
You need records for all of it, but the bar rises at $250. Below that, receipts and a mileage log are generally sufficient. At $250 or more for a single expense or a related group of expenses, you need a written acknowledgment from the organization obtained before you file.

**Can I deduct volunteer expenses if I take the standard deduction instead of itemizing?**
No. Volunteer expenses fall under charitable contributions, which only reduce your taxable income if you itemize deductions on Schedule A. If your total itemized deductions fall short of the standard deduction, the volunteer expenses won't provide any additional tax benefit that year.
