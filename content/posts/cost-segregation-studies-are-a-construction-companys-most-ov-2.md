If you run a construction company, you already own the exact kind of assets cost segregation was built for. Every truck, every piece of heavy equipment, every building you've put up or bought in the last several years is sitting on your depreciation schedule getting written off over 27.5 or 39 years like it's a generic office building. Most contractors never question that schedule. They assume cost segregation is something real estate investors do with apartment complexes, not something that applies to a construction business in Hartford, SD or anywhere else. That assumption costs money every year it goes unchallenged.

A cost segregation study is an engineering-based analysis that breaks a building or a large capital purchase into its component parts and assigns each part the depreciation life it actually deserves under IRS rules. Electrical work dedicated to specialized equipment, certain site improvements, interior finishes, and specific mechanical systems often qualify for 5, 7, or 15-year depreciation instead of the standard 39-year commercial schedule. For a construction company that self-performs work, buys equipment constantly, and often builds its own shop or office, that reclassification isn't a marginal benefit. It's a strategy that fits how the business already operates.

## Why construction companies fit this strategy better than most

Real estate investors get most of the attention around cost segregation, but they're often passive buyers of a single building type. Construction companies are different in three ways that matter for tax planning.

First, contractors typically own more categories of qualifying property: shops, yards, equipment barns, offices, and sometimes rental units, all acquired or built over time rather than purchased once. Second, many construction businesses self-perform a portion of their own facility work, which creates detailed cost records that make component identification easier and more defensible. Third, construction companies tend to run higher effective tax rates than the average small business because so much income flows through as ordinary income on a Schedule C, an 1120-S, or a partnership return, with fewer built-in deductions than a company that leases everything.

Put those three together and you get a business type that is structurally better positioned to benefit from a cost segregation study than the typical retail or professional services client. Yet in our experience advising contractors and agriculture operations across South Dakota, most owners have never had the conversation. They hear "cost segregation" and picture a hotel developer, not a paving contractor who just built a new 6,000-square-foot shop.

## Finding the money that's already on your balance sheet

A study doesn't create new deductions out of nothing. It accelerates deductions you were always entitled to, just spread across decades instead of concentrated in the years you actually need the cash. Think of a $1.2 million equipment building a construction company completes in year one. Under standard straight-line depreciation, that building generates roughly $30,000 a year in depreciation for 39 years. A cost segregation study might identify $250,000 to $350,000 of that cost as 5, 7, or 15-year property, things like specialized wiring for equipment bays, certain flooring systems, and site improvements around the yard.

That reclassified portion can then be depreciated far faster, and under bonus depreciation rules, a meaningful share of it may be eligible to be deducted in the very first year the property is placed in service. Instead of $30,000 in year-one depreciation, the business might see $150,000 to $200,000. For a contractor with a strong revenue year and a large tax bill on the horizon, that's not a rounding error. It's the difference between writing a five-figure check to the IRS and reinvesting that cash into equipment, payroll, or paying down a line of credit.

## Bonus depreciation is stepping down, and timing changes the math

Bonus depreciation has been on a scheduled phase-down since the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act provisions began stepping back: 80% in 2023, 60% in 2024, 40% in 2025, with further reductions scheduled after that unless Congress acts again. That schedule has shifted before and could shift again, which is exactly why this isn't a strategy to plan around a headline you read once. It's a strategy to revisit every year with a CPA who is actually tracking the current rate, not the rate from two tax seasons ago.

The practical impact is straightforward: the same cost segregation study performed in a year with a 60% bonus depreciation rate produces a bigger first-year deduction than one performed when the rate has dropped to 20%. Construction companies planning a new shop, a major equipment purchase, or a facility expansion have a real incentive to get the timing right, and that means looping in your accountant before the purchase closes, not after the return is already due.

## Self-performed work creates opportunities most contractors miss

Here's the part that's specific to construction and rarely discussed. When a contractor self-performs the build-out of their own facility, whether that's a general contractor building their own office or an excavation company grading and improving their own yard, the detailed job costing already required for the project becomes the backbone of the cost segregation study. Labor and material records that a construction company keeps as a matter of routine, the same records used for job costing and bid analysis, can substantially reduce the cost and time of a study because much of the component-level detail already exists.

This is where a lot of firms treat cost segregation as a one-size-fits-all compliance service. A contractor's own project records deserve a different approach, one that recognizes the business is sitting on documentation most other industries would have to reconstruct from scratch.

## Common Questions

**Does cost segregation only apply to buildings I purchase, or also to ones I build?**
Both. Newly constructed buildings, existing purchased commercial property, and even substantial renovations can qualify. Ground-up construction often produces the strongest results because a study can work directly from the actual construction cost records instead of estimating values after the fact.

**How much does a cost segregation study typically cost?**
Studies are usually priced based on the size and complexity of the property, often ranging from a few thousand dollars for a smaller facility to $10,000 or more for a large or multi-building project. The tax savings identified almost always exceed the study cost by a significant margin, which is why it's worth a real cost-benefit conversation before committing.

**Can I do a cost segregation study on a building I bought several years ago?**
Yes. This is called a look-back study, and it lets you claim the missed depreciation in the current tax year without amending prior returns, using a method called a change in accounting method. Buildings purchased or completed in the last 10 to 15 years are typically still good candidates.

**Will this trigger an IRS audit?**
A properly documented, engineering-based study performed according to IRS guidelines is a legitimate and well-established strategy, not a red flag on its own. The risk comes from poorly supported studies, which is exactly why the underlying documentation and methodology matter as much as the final numbers.
