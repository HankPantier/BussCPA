A portfolio built five years ago for a 45-year-old with a mortgage, two kids, and a growing agriculture operation doesn't fit that same person today. Tax brackets shift, interest rates move, a farm gets sold or expanded, retirement gets closer. Yet plenty of investors set an allocation once, walk away, and assume the market will sort itself out. It won't. Left alone, a portfolio drifts toward whatever asset class had the best run, which usually means it's carrying more risk than the investor ever signed up for.

This matters as much for a Hartford, SD business owner reinvesting profits as it does for a retiree living off a brokerage account. Money left unmanaged doesn't stay balanced. It stays wherever the market pushes it, and the market doesn't care about your goals.

## Why drift happens even when you don't touch a thing

Say you started with 60% stocks and 40% bonds. After a strong five-year stretch for equities, that same portfolio might sit at 75% stocks and 25% bonds without a single trade being made. Nobody decided to take on more risk. It happened because stocks grew faster than bonds, and nobody stepped in to correct it.

The Federal Reserve's research on household finance and investment behavior points to exactly this pattern: households that don't actively manage allocation tend to end up overexposed to whatever has recently performed well, right before it's most likely to correct. That's the opposite of buying low and selling high. It's buying high and staying high, then getting hit hardest when the cycle turns.

## Asset allocation strategy is a decision, not a default

A real asset allocation strategy accounts for three things: your time horizon, your risk tolerance, and your tax situation. Skip any one of those and the portfolio is guessing.

Time horizon is straightforward on paper but gets ignored in practice. Someone five years from retirement carries a different risk tolerance than someone twenty-five years out, even if both started with identical portfolios in their thirties. Risk tolerance investing isn't about how much risk you can stomach emotionally during a good year. It's about how much risk your actual timeline and cash flow needs can absorb during a bad one.

Tax situation is where a lot of individual investors and even some advisors leave money on the table. Rebalancing inside a 401(k) or IRA has no immediate tax consequence. Rebalancing inside a taxable brokerage account can trigger capital gains, and the difference between short-term and long-term rates is real money. A construction company owner with a taxable investment account and a busy season cash crunch needs a rebalancing plan that accounts for both, not one that treats the portfolio in isolation from the tax return.

## What portfolio diversification actually buys you

Diversification isn't about owning more things. It's about owning things that don't all move together. A portfolio split evenly between ten different tech stocks isn't diversified, it's concentrated with extra steps.

Genuine portfolio diversification spreads exposure across asset classes, sectors, and geographies so that a downturn in one area doesn't wipe out the whole account. For agriculture-connected families in South Dakota, this often means being deliberate about not stacking additional commodity-linked exposure on top of a farm operation that's already tied to grain and livestock prices. The operation itself is already a concentrated bet on agriculture. The investment portfolio doesn't need to double down on that same risk.

## Portfolio rebalancing on a schedule, not a whim

Most investors who do rebalance do it reactively, usually after a scare, which is the worst time to make allocation decisions. A disciplined portfolio rebalancing approach works on a calendar, typically annually or semi-annually, or on a threshold, triggering a rebalance any time an asset class drifts more than 5 percentage points from its target.

Here's a simple comparison of how those two approaches play out:

| Approach | Trigger | Typical frequency | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Calendar-based | Fixed date | 1-2x per year | Investors who want predictability |
| Threshold-based | Allocation drift beyond target | Variable | Investors comfortable monitoring more closely |

Either approach beats no plan at all. The AICPA's personal financial planning resources emphasize that rebalancing decisions should be made in coordination with a full financial picture, including tax brackets, upcoming liquidity needs, and estate considerations, not treated as a standalone investment decision.

## Where this connects to your tax return

A balanced investment portfolio doesn't live in a vacuum separate from your 1040. Selling appreciated assets to rebalance can push you into a higher capital gains bracket, trigger the net investment income tax, or interact badly with a year where you're also realizing gains from selling farmland, equipment, or a business interest. On the flip side, a down year in the market might be the right moment to harvest losses and offset gains elsewhere.

## Common Questions

**How often should I rebalance my investment portfolio?**
Most advisors recommend rebalancing annually or semi-annually, or whenever an asset class drifts more than 5 percentage points from its target allocation. The right frequency depends on your account type, since taxable accounts require more attention to the tax consequences of each trade than tax-deferred accounts like a 401(k) or IRA.

**What's the difference between asset allocation and diversification?**
Asset allocation is the split between broad categories like stocks, bonds, and cash. Diversification is how spread out your holdings are within and across those categories, so a downturn in one sector or asset class doesn't take down the whole portfolio.

**Does rebalancing trigger a tax bill?**
It can, if you're selling appreciated assets inside a taxable brokerage account. Rebalancing inside a 401(k) or IRA typically has no immediate tax consequence, which is why many investors rebalance tax-deferred accounts more freely than taxable ones.

**How does my business income affect my investment rebalancing decisions?**
A large gain or loss from selling business assets, equipment, or farmland can change your tax bracket for the year, which changes the cost of realizing additional gains in your investment portfolio. Coordinating the two means fewer surprises at tax time.
