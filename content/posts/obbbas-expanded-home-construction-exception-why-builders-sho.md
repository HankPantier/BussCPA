A framing crew breaks ground on a spec home outside Hartford in the spring of 2026. Under the old rules, that contract might have forced percentage-of-completion accounting, which means paying tax on profit while the house is still just a foundation and a stack of lumber. The One Big Beautiful Bill Act changed that calculus for a meaningful number of residential builders, and the change lands right as 2026 contracts start getting signed. If your construction company has been stuck reporting income before the cash from a draw or a closing actually arrives, this is worth ten minutes before your next contract goes out for signature.

## The rule builders have lived with for decades

Section 460 of the tax code requires long-term contracts, meaning any contract not completed in the tax year it's signed, to use the percentage-of-completion method (PCM) unless a specific exception applies. PCM recognizes income based on costs incurred to date compared to total estimated costs, regardless of whether the builder has been paid. Two exceptions have mattered for residential builders: the home construction contract exception under Section 460(e)(1)(A), and the small contractor exception tied to average annual gross receipts under Section 448(c).

The home construction exception itself hasn't changed much. A contract still qualifies if at least 80% of estimated costs relate to dwelling units in buildings with four or fewer units, or to improvements on the land those units sit on. What has changed is the gross receipts test that determines which contractors get relief even outside that narrow home construction definition, and that shift widens the pool of builders and developers who can skip PCM altogether.

## How the gross receipts threshold moved

Before this year, the small contractor exception under Section 460(e)(1)(B) applied only to contractors whose average annual gross receipts over the prior three years fell under roughly $31 million, an inflation-adjusted figure that had crept up slowly from the original $25 million base set back in 2017. [OBBBA raises that threshold to $80 million](/resources/obbbas-expanded-home-construction-exception-why-builders-sho-2), effective for contracts entered into after the law's enactment date. For a calendar-year construction company, that means the expanded exception applies in full to contracts signed starting in the 2026 tax year.

| | Prior law | Under OBBBA |
|---|---|---|
| Gross receipts threshold | Roughly $31 million (2025, indexed) | $80 million, indexed going forward |
| Applies to | Contracts entered before enactment | Contracts entered after enactment (2026 for calendar-year taxpayers) |
| Method allowed if exempt | Completed contract or other permissible method | Completed contract or other permissible method |

A general contractor doing $45 million in average annual revenue was locked into PCM on non-home-construction jobs before this change. Under the new threshold, that same contractor now qualifies for the small contractor exception and can choose completed contract accounting for eligible long-term contracts instead.

## Why the timing of income has real cash flow consequences

Percentage-of-completion accounting doesn't change how much profit a job eventually makes. It changes when that profit shows up on a tax return, and that timing difference is where builders feel the pinch. Consider a $1.8 million custom home contract running 14 months, spanning two tax years. Under PCM, if 55% of estimated costs are incurred by the end of year one, the builder recognizes roughly 55% of the projected profit that same year, even though the home hasn't closed and the final draw hasn't hit the bank account. Under completed contract accounting, none of that profit gets taxed until the home is substantially complete and sold, typically in year two.

That gap matters most for builders financing land, materials, and subcontractor payments out of pocket while waiting on draws. Paying tax on income you haven't collected yet is a cash flow problem dressed up as an accounting rule. For homebuilders and developers running several projects at once, the difference between PCM and completed contract accounting across a portfolio of contracts can shift six figures of taxable income between tax years.

## Building a method-change strategy before contracts lock in

The exception applies based on when a contract is entered into, not when a builder decides to change accounting methods. That means existing contracts signed before enactment stay on whatever method the builder was already using, while new contracts signed in 2026 and beyond can take advantage of the wider exception. A few things worth working through with your CPA before bid season ramps up:

- Recalculate the three-year average gross receipts test now, using actual numbers, not last year's estimate. Related entities under common control, such as a separate land development LLC or equipment leasing company tied to the same ownership group, generally get aggregated for this test.
- Decide which contracts, if any, should switch from PCM to completed contract or another permissible method, and file Form 3115 to request the change. Most of these changes qualify as automatic changes, but the Section 481(a) adjustment still needs to be calculated correctly.
- Model both scenarios before signing new contracts. A builder near the $80 million threshold in either direction needs to know which side of the line they'll land on before committing to a method for a multi-year project.
- If any contracts remain on PCM, keep the look-back method calculations current. The [IRS Instructions for Form 8697](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i8697) walk through how interest gets computed when estimated and actual contract income diverge, and that filing obligation doesn't disappear just because other contracts moved to completed contract accounting.

The [AICPA's tax resources](https://www.aicpa-cima.com/topic/tax) are a useful starting point for the underlying guidance, but the aggregation rules and the 481(a) mechanics are where builders tend to get tripped up without a CPA who works construction contracts regularly.

## Common Questions

**Does the expanded home construction exception mean all residential builders skip percentage-of-completion now?**
No. The exception applies to specific contracts, either ones meeting the 80% dwelling-unit test under Section 460(e)(1)(A) or contracts from builders under the $80 million average gross receipts threshold. Commercial contracts and larger builders above that threshold still generally use PCM.

**What's the new gross receipts threshold under OBBBA?**
$80 million in average annual gross receipts over the prior three tax years, up from roughly $31 million under prior law, applying to contracts entered into after the law's enactment date, which means 2026 for calendar-year taxpayers.

**Can I switch existing contracts to completed contract accounting right now?**
Generally no. The exception is tied to when a contract is entered into, not when you decide to change methods. Contracts signed before OBBBA's enactment date typically stay on the method they started with, while new contracts going forward can use the expanded exception.

**Do I need IRS approval to change accounting methods for construction contracts?**
Yes. Most builders file Form 3115 to request an automatic change and calculate a Section 481(a) adjustment for the transition. Getting that adjustment right, along with the gross receipts aggregation rules for related entities, is where a construction-focused CPA earns their fee.
