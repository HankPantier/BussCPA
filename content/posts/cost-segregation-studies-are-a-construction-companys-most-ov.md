A construction company that just poured the foundation for a new equipment shop, added a paint booth, or built out a shop office rarely thinks about cost segregation. That term gets filed under "real estate investor tax strategy" and forgotten. It shouldn't be. Contractors and builders sit on exactly the kind of fixed assets that [make cost segregation studies pay off](/resources/cost-segregation-studies-are-a-construction-companys-most-ov-2), and most are leaving five and six figures of tax deferral on the table every year they skip it.

Cost segregation is an engineering-based study that breaks a building or improvement into its component parts and reclassifies pieces of it from 39-year (or 27.5-year residential) property into 5-year, 7-year, and 15-year property. Wiring dedicated to specific equipment, specialty plumbing, paving, site lighting, and interior partitions often qualify for the shorter recovery periods. Shorter recovery period means faster depreciation, which means a lower tax bill now instead of spread thin over four decades.

## Why construction companies fit this strategy better than most

Real estate investors get pitched cost segregation constantly because they buy finished buildings. Construction companies are in a stronger position for a different reason: they build the asset themselves, or self-perform significant portions of the work, which means detailed job costing records already exist. Labor hours, material invoices, and subcontractor draws are broken out by task rather than lumped into one purchase price. That documentation is exactly what a cost segregation study needs to substantiate reclassification, and it's far more defensible than an estimate applied after the fact to a purchased building.

Construction firms also tend to carry a heavier mix of qualifying assets than a typical office-based business: shop buildings, fabrication bays, wash bays, fuel islands, yard lighting, fencing, and paving around equipment storage. Each of those is a candidate for a shorter depreciation life. A general contractor who just finished a $1.2 million pole building and yard improvement project in eastern South Dakota might reasonably expect 20% to 35% of that cost to be reclassified out of 39-year property, depending on the mix of site work, electrical, and specialty systems involved.

Add to that the effective tax rate contractors typically carry, often higher than other small businesses because of strong cash flow years tied to project completions, and the case gets stronger. A construction company with a profitable year and a large capital project sitting in the same twelve months is often the single best candidate for a cost segregation study that a CPA firm will see all year.

## What actually gets reclassified

A study doesn't touch the structural shell, the roof, or the foundation. Those stay on the standard schedule. What moves are the components that support specific business functions rather than the building itself.

| Asset type | Typical recovery period without study | Recovery period after reclassification |
|---|---|---|
| Structural shell, roof, foundation | 39 years | 39 years (unchanged) |
| Dedicated equipment wiring and outlets | 39 years | 5 or 7 years |
| Specialty plumbing for wash bays or paint booths | 39 years | 7 or 15 years |
| Interior partitions, non-structural walls | 39 years | 7 years |
| Site lighting, fencing, security systems | 39 years | 15 years |
| Paving, curbing, storm drainage | 39 years | 15 years |

The dollar amounts add up fast on a construction company's balance sheet because these are the categories construction companies build in bulk. A contractor who self-performs site work already has the cost breakdown in the job file. That's a meaningful head start compared to a business that bought a completed building with one lump-sum purchase price on the closing statement.

## Bonus depreciation makes timing matter right now

Cost segregation and bonus depreciation work together, and the bonus depreciation percentage is dropping fast. Property with a recovery period of 20 years or less, the exact category cost segregation creates, qualifies for bonus depreciation in the year it's placed in service. The schedule under current law looks like this:

| Year placed in service | Bonus depreciation rate |
|---|---|
| 2022 | 100% |
| 2023 | 80% |
| 2024 | 60% |
| 2025 | 40% |
| 2026 | 20% |
| 2027 | 0% |

A construction company that completes a new facility in 2025 and reclassifies $300,000 into 5, 7, and 15-year property can deduct 40% of that amount, $120,000, immediately, on top of standard first-year depreciation on the remaining balance. Wait until 2027 and that same project gets no bonus depreciation benefit at all. The window to capture meaningful acceleration is closing year by year, which makes this a planning conversation to have before year-end, not after the return is already filed.

## Where the real savings show up

The tax deferral isn't the only benefit, though it's usually the one that gets a contractor's attention first. Faster depreciation improves cash flow in years when equipment purchases, bonding requirements, or seasonal payroll swings are already putting pressure on the checking account. For a construction company carrying agriculture-adjacent work, where payroll and tax timing already differ from a typical commercial job, freeing up cash through accelerated depreciation can smooth out a rough quarter without touching a line of credit.

A cost segregation study also creates a documented asset schedule that holds up if the business is ever sold, refinanced, or if a piece of property is disposed of early. Knowing the specific depreciated basis of a paint booth or a section of yard paving matters when that component gets replaced or the building changes hands.

## Common Questions

**Does cost segregation only make sense for new construction?**
No. A study can be done on a building purchased or placed in service in a prior year through a process called a look-back study, which captures the missed depreciation in the current year without amending prior returns. Renovations and additions qualify too, not just ground-up builds.

**How much does a construction company typically save?**
It depends on the project mix, but a $1 million commercial or industrial building with meaningful site work often sees 20% to 35% of the cost reclassified into shorter recovery periods. At current bonus depreciation rates, that can translate into a five-figure or six-figure reduction in current-year taxable income depending on the project size and the company's tax bracket.

**Is a cost segregation study going to trigger an audit?**
An engineering-based study, properly documented and tied to actual construction costs, is a standard and well-established tax position. The risk isn't the strategy itself, it's doing a rough estimate instead of a defensible study backed by real cost detail, which is exactly the kind of documentation a self-performing contractor already has on hand.

**Should agriculture or construction clients handle this differently?**
The mechanics are the same, but the timing conversation isn't. Agriculture-adjacent construction work often runs on a different payroll and tax calendar, so the decision about when to place a building in service and how it interacts with other equipment purchases in the same year needs to be planned alongside the rest of the operation, not treated as a separate tax event.
