A GMC Sierra sitting on a Hartford dealership lot doesn't care whether you lease it or finance it. The salesperson pushing you toward one option over the other usually cares more about moving inventory and hitting a commission target than about your tax return or your cash flow twelve months from now. That's the gap a CPA fills: looking at your specific numbers instead of a payment that sounds good on a Tuesday afternoon test drive.

Whether you're an individual in Minnehaha County replacing a family vehicle or a contractor adding a truck to your fleet, [the lease-versus-buy decision comes down to four things](/resources/auto-leasing-vs-buying-which-makes-more-sense-2): how you use the vehicle, how long you plan to keep it, what your tax situation looks like, and what it does to your monthly cash flow. Get those four right and the decision usually makes itself.

## Why the monthly payment is the wrong starting point

Dealerships sell payments. A $429-a-month lease looks better than a $612-a-month loan payment on paper, but that comparison ignores what you own at the end of the term, how mileage penalties stack up, and whether the vehicle qualifies for a business deduction at all. A lease with a 12,000-mile annual cap and a $0.25-per-mile overage fee can turn into a $2,000 surprise if you're a construction superintendent driving between three job sites in Lincoln County every week.

Start instead with total cost of use over the period you'd actually keep the vehicle, usually three to seven years. That includes the payment, the down payment or drive-off fees, maintenance responsibility, insurance differences, and what you'd walk away with (equity in a purchase, nothing in a lease unless you buy it out).

## Leasing and buying compared side by side

| Factor | Leasing | Buying |
|---|---|---|
| Upfront cost | Lower, often just first month plus fees | Higher, down payment or full price |
| Monthly payment | Typically lower | Typically higher |
| Mileage limits | Yes, usually 10,000–15,000/year | None |
| Equity built | None | Yes, once loan is paid off |
| Maintenance | Often covered under warranty for lease term | Owner's responsibility after warranty |
| Business tax deduction | Lease payments deducted as expense | Depreciation, Section 179, or standard mileage rate |
| Best for | Vehicles swapped every 2–3 years, high-mileage business use with no ownership goal | Vehicles kept 5+ years, heavy annual mileage, want to build equity |

## Section 179 and bonus depreciation for business vehicles

This is where the decision changes for self-employed professionals, agriculture operators, and construction business owners. If you buy a vehicle and use it more than 50% for business, you may be able to deduct a significant portion of the purchase price in the year you place it in service, rather than depreciating it over five or six years.

[IRS Publication 946](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p946) lays out the rules for Section 179 expensing and bonus depreciation, including the special limitation that applies to SUVs and trucks over 6,000 pounds gross vehicle weight rating. Heavy pickups commonly used in construction and agriculture often qualify for a larger first-year deduction than a sedan or a lighter SUV, but the exact dollar caps change year to year, so confirm the current-year figures before you commit to a purchase for tax reasons alone.

Leased vehicles don't get this treatment. You deduct the business-use portion of the lease payment as an ordinary expense, spread evenly across the term. That's simpler, but it rarely produces the same first-year tax benefit that a Section 179-eligible purchase can, particularly for a farm operation buying a truck outright before year-end to offset a strong grain sale.

## Where leasing wins

Leasing tends to make sense when:

- You or your business replaces vehicles every two to three years and don't want to manage resale or trade-in value.
- Your annual mileage is predictable and stays under the lease cap.
- You want lower monthly payments to preserve cash for equipment, payroll, or inventory instead of tying it up in a vehicle.
- You run a professional practice, like a law office or engineering firm, where a newer vehicle matters for client-facing work but heavy hauling or job-site wear isn't part of the equation.

Attorneys and engineers we work with often fall into this category. The vehicle is a tool for meetings and site visits, not a piece of production equipment, and the tax deduction math favors simplicity over maximizing depreciation.

## Where buying wins

Buying tends to make sense when:

- You put high mileage on the vehicle, which is common in agriculture and construction, where trucks routinely exceed 20,000 miles a year hauling equipment or livestock.
- You plan to keep the vehicle five years or longer, past the point where a lease would have required a new agreement anyway.
- You want the Section 179 or bonus depreciation deduction against a profitable year, and IRS Publication 946 confirms the vehicle and business-use percentage qualify.
- You want an asset on the balance sheet with resale value instead of a liability that disappears at lease-end.

For a Hartford-area farm buying a new grain truck ahead of harvest, ownership almost always wins once you factor in unlimited mileage and the depreciation benefit. For a real estate agent who wants a fresh vehicle every two years and puts modest mileage on it, leasing often comes out ahead.

## Cash flow and financing conditions matter too

Interest rates on auto loans move with the broader credit market, and the [Federal Reserve's Consumer Credit report](https://www.federalreserve.gov/releases/g19/current/) tracks how auto loan balances and terms shift nationally. When rates run higher, the gap between a lease payment and a loan payment widens, and that changes the breakeven point for how long you need to keep a purchased vehicle to come out ahead of leasing. This is a moving target, not a one-time calculation, which is part of why this decision deserves a real conversation rather than a dealership finance office spreadsheet.

Cash flow discipline matters as much as the tax deduction. A construction company that finances three trucks at once to chase Section 179 savings but strains its working capital in the process hasn't made a good decision, it's made a tax-motivated mistake. We've seen owners get so focused on the deduction that they overlook whether the payment fits comfortably into monthly cash flow. If a vehicle purchase pressures payroll or job costing, the tax savings won't cover the gap.

## How agriculture and construction clients typically approach this

Agriculture clients face payroll and tax timing that differs from most industries, and vehicle purchases often get planned around harvest income and equipment depreciation schedules already in place. A truck bought in October ahead of grain sales income can shift tax liability meaningfully, but only if the purchase and financing terms are set up correctly before year-end, not scrambled together in December.

Construction companies weighing a fleet purchase should also look at how vehicle depreciation interacts with other capital investments already on the books. If your company owns commercial buildings or equipment, a [cost segregation study](/resources/cost-segregation-studies-are-a-construction-companys-most-ov) may already be accelerating depreciation elsewhere, and stacking a large vehicle deduction in the same year can create a bigger loss than you actually want for loan covenant or bonding purposes.

## Common Questions

**Is it better to lease or buy a car for tax purposes?**
It depends on business-use percentage and how long you keep the vehicle. Buying opens the door to Section 179 and bonus depreciation under [IRS Publication 946](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p946), which can produce a larger first-year deduction than deducting lease payments, but only if the vehicle qualifies and the business use supports it.

**Can I take the Section 179 deduction on a leased vehicle?**
No. Section 179 applies to vehicles you purchase and place in service, not to leased vehicles. Lease payments are deducted as an ordinary business expense instead, spread across the lease term based on business-use percentage.

**Does leasing or buying make more sense for a farm truck?**
Most agriculture operations come out ahead buying, since farm trucks typically rack up high annual mileage well beyond standard lease limits, and a purchased heavy truck may qualify for accelerated depreciation that fits well against a strong harvest income year.

**How do interest rates affect the lease versus buy decision?**
Higher auto loan rates increase the monthly cost of financing a purchase, narrowing or eliminating the cash flow advantage leasing typically offers. The [Federal Reserve's Consumer Credit report](https://www.federalreserve.gov/releases/g19/current/) tracks these trends and is worth checking before locking in loan terms.
