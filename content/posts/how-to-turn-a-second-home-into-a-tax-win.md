A cabin on Lake Madison or a place in the Black Hills sounds like a lifestyle upgrade, and it is. But the IRS doesn't see a lifestyle upgrade. It sees a set of days: how many nights you slept there yourself, and how many nights you rented it to someone else. That single ratio decides whether your second home functions as a quiet tax shelter, a fully taxable rental business, or something in between. Get the days wrong and you can lose deductions you didn't know you had, or owe tax on income you thought was free. Get them right, and a property you already wanted becomes a genuinely smart financial move.

## The 14-day rule most owners never hear about

Here's the rule real estate agents rarely mention: if you rent out your second home for 14 days or fewer during the year, the rental income is completely tax-free. You don't report it. You don't owe self-employment tax on it. You don't file a Schedule E. This applies even if a week of rental income at market rate covers your property taxes for the year.

The catch is the reverse side of that same rule. Rent the property for 15 days or more, and every dollar of that income becomes reportable, whether you use the home yourself for two weeks or two months. At that point, the second question kicks in: how many days did you personally use the place? The IRS defines personal use broadly. It includes time used by you, your family members, or anyone paying less than fair market rent, per [IRS Publication 527](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p527). A week spent up at the lake with your kids counts as personal use even if you never charged yourself rent.

## Three tax categories, three different outcomes

Once you're past the 14-day exemption, the IRS sorts your property into one of two remaining buckets based on personal use relative to rental use, and each bucket changes what you can deduct.

| Category | Days test | What it means for you |
|---|---|---|
| Tax-free personal residence | Rented 14 days or fewer per year | Rental income excluded entirely; no rental expense deductions allowed |
| Vacation home with mixed use | Personal use exceeds the greater of 14 days or 10% of rental days, and rented 15+ days | Rental income and expenses reported on Schedule E, but deductible expenses capped at rental income (no loss allowed) |
| Rental property | Personal use is 14 days or fewer, or under 10% of rental days | Reported like a standard rental; losses may be deductible depending on your income and participation |

The middle category catches most second-home owners who rent out a lake place or ski condo for a few weeks a summer or winter but still use it themselves throughout the season. You get to deduct mortgage interest, property taxes, utilities, and depreciation, but only up to the amount of rental income you collected. Any excess expense carries forward instead of creating a current-year loss. Details on how the IRS splits these expenses between personal and rental days are laid out in [IRS Tax Topic 415](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc415).

## Second home vs investment property: the line that changes your deductions

The words "second home" and "investment property" get used interchangeably in real estate listings, but they trigger different tax rules entirely.

A second home, in IRS terms, is a property you use personally enough of the year to clear the thresholds above. Mortgage interest on a second home is deductible under the same rules as your primary residence, subject to the $750,000 combined mortgage debt limit for loans taken out after December 15, 2017. Property taxes fall under the $10,000 SALT cap that already applies to your primary home.

An investment property, by contrast, is one where personal use stays under the 14-day or 10% threshold. Mortgage interest becomes a rental business expense on Schedule E instead of an itemized deduction, there's no SALT cap on the property tax portion, and you're now depreciating the structure over 27.5 years. That depreciation shelters income now, but it comes back to bite you at sale through depreciation recapture, taxed at up to 25%.

## Turning rental days into a tax advantage without losing personal use

The sweet spot for a lot of second-home owners is [staying just under the personal-use threshold](/resources/how-to-turn-a-second-home-into-a-tax-win-2) while renting enough days to generate real income and real deductions. If you own a place near Big Stone Lake and use it for three long weekends a summer, that's roughly 9 to 12 personal days. Rent it for another 20 to 30 days during peak weeks, and you likely land in rental property territory rather than the mixed-use category, meaning losses from depreciation and expenses can actually offset other income, subject to passive activity limits.

The passive activity rules allow up to $25,000 in rental losses to offset ordinary income if you actively participate in managing the property and your modified adjusted gross income is under $100,000, phasing out completely at $150,000. Above that threshold, losses suspend and carry forward until you sell or generate passive income to absorb them.

None of this works without a clean log. Track every day the property is used, by whom, and whether rent was charged at fair market value. A missed week of documented personal use can shift you from one category to another and change your entire return.

## What South Dakota second-home owners should watch for

Second homes tied to South Dakota's lake country and Black Hills tourism corridor often have seasonal rental patterns that make the day-count rules especially relevant. A cabin rented heavily during pheasant season or the summer lake months, then closed up the rest of the year, can easily land in the investment property category even with a handful of personal weekends. That opens the door to depreciation and full expense deductions, but it also means self-employment considerations if you're providing hotel-like services such as daily cleaning or meals, which can push the activity from passive rental into an active trade or business.

If the property sits on land also used for hunting leases or agricultural purposes, the accounting gets layered further. Agricultural income carries its own payroll and tax treatment that doesn't match a straightforward vacation rental, and mixing the two on one return without separating them cleanly is a common source of errors.

## Depreciation, expenses, and the passive loss trap

Once a property qualifies as a rental, you're required to depreciate it, even in years you'd rather not claim the deduction. Skipping depreciation doesn't avoid recapture at sale. The IRS calculates recapture based on depreciation allowed or allowable, so failing to claim it just means paying the recapture tax without having gotten the benefit along the way.

Expenses need to be allocated proportionally between personal and rental days for mixed-use properties. Mortgage interest and property taxes are typically allocated based on total days used (personal plus rental), while other expenses like utilities and maintenance follow rental days only. Getting this allocation wrong is one of the more common triggers for an IRS inquiry on Schedule E filings.

A second home can function as both a family retreat and a tax-efficient asset, but only if the day counts, expense allocations, and depreciation schedule are handled deliberately from year one. Retrofitting three years of records after the fact is possible, but it costs more in time and CPA fees than getting it right from the purchase date.

## Common Questions

**What is the IRS 14-day rule for second homes?**
If you rent your second home for 14 days or fewer in a calendar year, the rental income is entirely tax-free and doesn't need to be reported, regardless of the rental rate charged, per [IRS Publication 527](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p527).

**Is my lake cabin a second home or an investment property for tax purposes?**
It depends on personal use. If your personal use exceeds 14 days or 10% of the days it's rented, whichever is greater, the IRS treats it as a second home with mixed use. Stay under that threshold and it's classified as a rental property with different deduction rules.

**Can I deduct a loss on my vacation rental?**
Only if the property qualifies as a rental (personal use under the 14-day/10% threshold) rather than a mixed-use vacation home. Mixed-use properties can deduct expenses only up to rental income received, per [IRS Tax Topic 415](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc415).

**Do I need to report rental income if I rent my second home for less than two weeks?**
No. Under the 14-day rule, that income is excluded from your tax return entirely, and you also can't deduct rental expenses tied to those days.
