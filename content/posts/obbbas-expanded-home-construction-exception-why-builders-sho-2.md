A homebuilder working spec homes across Minnehaha and Turner counties has likely used percentage-of-completion accounting for years, not by choice but because the gross receipts threshold left no other option. That threshold just moved, and it moved a lot. The One Big Beautiful Bill Act expands the home construction exception under IRC Section 460, and the change reaches contracts signed as early as 2026 for calendar-year builders. If you sign new contracts this year without checking where you now land under the revised rules, you could lock yourself into a method you no longer need.

This matters most for construction companies, developers, and contractors already on our radar in Hartford and across South Dakota. The mechanics are technical, but the cash flow consequences are not: this is about when you pay tax on work you haven't been paid for yet.

## What OBBBA changed for residential contractors

Section 460 generally requires long-term contracts to use the percentage-of-completion method, recognizing income as a project progresses rather than when it's finished. Home construction contracts have always had a carve-out from that rule, but only for contractors under a specific average annual gross receipts test. Anyone above that line had to use percentage-of-completion whether it fit their cash flow or not.

OBBBA raises the gross receipts threshold to $80 million in average annual gross receipts over the prior three tax years, up from roughly $31 million under prior law. A home construction contract still has to meet the existing definition: at least 80% of estimated total contract costs must relate to building, reconstructing, or improving dwelling units, or to real property directly connected to those units. But far more builders and developers now fall under the exception's income cap, which means far more of them can use the completed contract method or another exempt method instead of percentage-of-completion.

## The new threshold in plain numbers

| | Prior law | OBBBA |
|---|---|---|
| Average annual gross receipts test | About $31 million (indexed) | $80 million |
| Who typically qualifies now | Small contractors only | Most mid-size builders and residential developers |
| Look-back period for the test | 3 prior tax years | 3 prior tax years, unchanged |
| Contract definition | 80% dwelling-unit cost test | Unchanged |

A builder with $45 million in average gross receipts was required to use percentage-of-completion under prior law. That same builder now sits comfortably under the $80 million line and can consider the completed contract method for qualifying home construction contracts.

## Why percentage-of-completion has been costing builders cash

Percentage-of-completion forces you to recognize income as costs are incurred, not when the home sells or the draw comes in. On a 10-month build, that means paying tax on revenue tied up in framing, drywall, and finish work months before a closing check arrives. For a contractor running five or six projects at once, the tax bill can outrun the cash in the bank by a wide margin.

The completed contract method defers both income and costs until the contract is substantially complete. Tax liability lines up with the year you actually get paid. For builders and developers who've felt the squeeze of paying tax on work-in-progress, this is the practical benefit behind the technical rule change. It's also worth pairing a method review with a look at your fixed asset strategy: builders holding equipment, structures, or land improvements often leave depreciation on the table, a topic we cover in [our piece on cost segregation studies for construction companies](/resources/cost-segregation-studies-are-a-construction-companys-most-ov-2).

## Making the switch to completed contract accounting

Switching from percentage-of-completion to the completed contract method is a formal accounting method change, not a footnote on next year's return. It requires filing Form 3115 with the IRS and generally applies on a cutoff basis to new contracts rather than restating work already in progress. The mechanics matter: get the effective date wrong, or file after contracts are already signed under the old assumption, and you can end up straddling two methods for years.

The AICPA's tax resources are a useful starting point for understanding how method changes interact with existing IRS guidance, though every builder's contract mix is different enough that a generic checklist won't catch everything. Reviewing your three-year average gross receipts, your contract definitions, and your existing method election before you file is the part that actually determines whether the switch saves money.

## Timing matters before your 2026 contracts are signed

The expanded exception applies to contracts entered into after OBBBA's enactment date, which for a calendar-year builder means essentially every contract signed in 2026. That's the urgency here. If you sign a development-phase contract or a spec home agreement in January without first confirming whether you now qualify for the exception, you may lock that specific contract into percentage-of-completion for its entire life, even though a contract signed a month later, after a method change is in place, would have qualified for deferral.

This is not a decision to make contract by contract as they come across your desk. It needs to happen before your 2026 contract calendar fills up, with a clear read on your trailing three-year gross receipts average and a plan for which contracts the completed contract method will actually apply to.

## What to watch before you file Form 3115

Contracts that remain on percentage-of-completion, whether by choice or because they don't meet the home construction definition, still carry the look-back method requirements under Section 460. If you've overreported or underreported income in prior years on long-term contracts, you may need to true that up using the [IRS Form 8697 instructions](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i8697) for the look-back method on long-term contracts. That calculation doesn't disappear just because your newer contracts qualify for the exception; it still applies to anything still governed by percentage-of-completion.

Also confirm your contract definitions hold up. A development contract that mixes dwelling-unit construction with commercial infrastructure, road work, or utility installation for a subdivision may not clear the 80% cost test even if the rest of your portfolio does. Sorting qualifying contracts from non-qualifying ones before you file is not optional paperwork, it's the difference between a clean method change and an IRS notice two years from now.

## Common Questions

**Does the home construction exception apply to spec homes, or only contracts with a signed buyer?**
It applies to home construction contracts as defined under Section 460, which covers building, reconstructing, or substantially improving dwelling units regardless of whether a buyer is under contract when construction starts. Spec homes generally qualify as long as at least 80% of estimated costs relate to the dwelling units themselves.

**What's the real difference between percentage-of-completion and the completed contract method?**
Percentage-of-completion recognizes income and costs as work progresses, so you pay tax on revenue before the project closes. The completed contract method defers both income and costs until the contract is substantially finished, which usually means the tax bill lands closer to when you actually collect payment.

**Do I need to file Form 3115 to switch methods, and when should I do it?**
Yes. Switching from percentage-of-completion to the completed contract method, or to another exempt method, is a formal IRS accounting method change requiring Form 3115. It needs to be filed with a clear cutoff date, ideally before you sign contracts you intend to have covered under the new method.

**Does this change affect agriculture-related development or land contracts?**
It can, if the contract meets the dwelling-unit cost test, which matters for agricultural landowners moving into residential development or rural subdivision work. Agriculture clients often have payroll and tax situations that don't map cleanly onto standard construction rules, so this is worth reviewing contract by contract rather than assuming one answer fits the whole operation.
