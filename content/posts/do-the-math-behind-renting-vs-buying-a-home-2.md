A homebuyer in Hartford, SD asking whether to buy or keep renting usually starts with the wrong question. They ask what the market is doing, or whether rates will drop next year, or what their neighbor paid for a similar house. None of that matters as much as one thing: what does this decision do to your personal balance sheet and cash flow over the next five to ten years? Buying a home is a leveraged financial transaction, not a lifestyle milestone, and it deserves the same numbers-first treatment you'd give any other major capital decision.

This is where a lot of good financial planning gets skipped. People run a mortgage calculator, look at the monthly payment next to their rent, and call it analysis. That comparison misses maintenance, insurance, opportunity cost on the down payment, and the tax mechanics that actually shift the math one way or the other. Below is the framework we walk clients through at BussCPA, whether they're buying their first house in Minnehaha County or deciding whether to keep renting while they build a construction business.

## Start with the true monthly cost, not the sticker payment

A mortgage quote shows principal and interest. It rarely shows the full carrying cost of ownership. Add property taxes, homeowners insurance, private mortgage insurance if the down payment is under 20%, and a realistic maintenance reserve, typically 1% to 2% of the home's value per year. A $300,000 house can easily carry $400 to $500 a month in costs that never show up on the loan estimate.

Renters have a simpler, more predictable cost structure: rent plus renters insurance, usually under $20 a month. That predictability has value. It also means renters avoid the surprise costs of a failed furnace or a roof replacement, expenses that hit owners with no warning and no ability to spread out over twelve months.

| Cost category | Owning | Renting |
|---|---|---|
| Principal and interest | Yes | No |
| Property tax | Yes | Built into rent |
| Insurance | Homeowners, ~$100-150/mo | Renters, ~$15-20/mo |
| Maintenance reserve | 1-2% of value/year | Landlord's responsibility |
| Down payment opportunity cost | Yes | No |

## Factor in what the down payment could have earned elsewhere

A 20% down payment on a $300,000 home is $60,000 that stops being liquid and stops earning a market return the moment it goes into escrow. That money could otherwise sit in a diversified portfolio. According to the Federal Reserve's [Survey of Consumer Finances](https://www.federalreserve.gov/publications/files/scf20.pdf), home equity makes up a substantial share of net worth for most American households, but it's also one of the least liquid assets a family holds. Locking up $60,000 has an opportunity cost even if the house appreciates, because that capital can't be redeployed into a brokerage account, a business, or an emergency fund.

This doesn't mean renting wins by default. It means the opportunity cost of the down payment needs to sit in the same spreadsheet as the mortgage interest deduction, appreciation assumptions, and rent inflation. Skip that step and the comparison is incomplete before it starts.

## The mortgage interest deduction rarely tips the scale the way people assume

Many homebuyers assume the mortgage interest deduction makes ownership dramatically cheaper. For most households, it doesn't move the needle as much as it used to. The IRS allows a deduction on mortgage interest under [Topic No. 505](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc505), but that benefit only matters if itemized deductions exceed the standard deduction, and for a lot of households they don't come close. A couple with a $250,000 mortgage at 6.5% pays roughly $16,000 in interest in year one. If their standard deduction already covers more than that once combined with other itemized items, the marginal tax benefit of buying can be small or nonexistent.

This is the kind of detail that separates a real financial plan from a rule of thumb picked up at a dinner party. Run your own numbers against your actual filing status and other itemized deductions before assuming ownership buys you a meaningful tax break.

## Compare the break-even timeline, not just the monthly payment

Buying a home comes with transaction costs on both ends: closing costs of 2% to 5% on the purchase, and realtor commissions plus closing costs of roughly 8% to 10% if you sell. Those costs mean a buyer typically needs to stay in a home for at least three to five years before ownership beats renting on a pure cost basis, assuming modest appreciation. Move sooner than that, and the transaction costs alone can wipe out any equity gained.

Anyone who expects a job change, a growing family that outgrows the house, or uncertainty about staying in the Hartford area within the next few years should weigh that timeline heavily. Renting keeps optionality. Buying trades optionality for equity building, and that trade only pays off if the time horizon supports it.

## Run the numbers against your actual household, not a national average

Generic rent-vs-buy calculators use national averages for appreciation, rent growth, and investment returns. Your household isn't a national average. A family with irregular income, like many agriculture households in the area managing seasonal cash flow, needs a different break-even analysis than a salaried engineer with predictable paychecks. Someone running a construction business with variable monthly draws should stress-test the mortgage payment against a lean month, not an average month.

A home purchase doesn't happen in isolation. It affects how much you can contribute to retirement accounts, how exposed you are if income drops for a quarter or two, and how your [investment portfolio](/resources/strategically-balancing-your-investment-portfolio-why-set-it-2) should be allocated once a large chunk of net worth sits in a single illiquid asset. ## Common Questions

**Is renting better than buying financially?**
It depends on how long you plan to stay, your down payment's opportunity cost, and whether your itemized deductions exceed the standard deduction. Renting often wins financially for stays under three to five years or in high-price markets with low rent-to-price ratios; buying tends to win over longer horizons once transaction costs are amortized.

**How do I calculate the true cost of buying vs renting a home?**
Add mortgage principal and interest, property tax, insurance, and a 1-2% annual maintenance reserve, then subtract any realistic tax benefit and add the opportunity cost of your down payment. Compare that total to your all-in rent plus renters insurance over the same time horizon.

**Does the mortgage interest deduction make buying cheaper?**
Only if your itemized deductions, including mortgage interest under [IRS Topic No. 505](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc505), exceed your standard deduction. For many households the standard deduction already covers more, which reduces or eliminates the tax advantage of owning.

**How long do I need to stay in a home to make buying worth it?**
Most buyers need three to five years to offset closing costs and selling costs through equity and appreciation. Moving sooner often means the transaction costs outweigh any financial benefit of ownership.
