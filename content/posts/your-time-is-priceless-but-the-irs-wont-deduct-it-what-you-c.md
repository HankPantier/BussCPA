A Hartford client who spends every Saturday coaching youth wrestling once asked us if he could deduct his time. He puts in close to 200 hours a season, and by any hourly rate he'd charge a client, that's real money walking out the door. The answer is no. The IRS has never allowed a deduction for the value of volunteer time, no matter the skill involved or the hours logged, but [understanding what the IRS will deduct for volunteering](/resources/your-time-is-priceless-but-the-irs-wont-deduct-it-what-you-c-2) can help you capture missed deductions. But the mileage he drives to tournaments, the uniforms he buys for kids whose families can't afford them, and the entry fees he covers out of pocket? Those can be deductible, and most people who volunteer never claim them.

This mix-up costs taxpayers money every year, mostly because the rules sit in an odd middle ground. Your time is worth nothing to the IRS. Your wallet is worth something, as long as the spending was for a qualified organization and you didn't get anything back for it. Understanding where that line falls is the difference between a bigger refund and a missed deduction you'll never get back.

## Why your hours don't count but your dollars might

The IRS treats volunteer labor the same way it treats a gift of services generally: no deduction, ever. A CPA who prepares taxes for free for a local food shelf can't deduct what she'd normally bill for that work. A contractor who frames a Habitat for Humanity house on his weekends can't deduct his labor rate either, even though the finished product has obvious market value.

What changes the math is cash spent in service of the volunteer work. According to [IRS Publication 526](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p526), unreimbursed expenses you pay to do volunteer work for a qualified organization can be deductible as a charitable contribution, provided three things are true: the organization qualifies under IRS rules, you weren't reimbursed, and the expense connects directly to the volunteer service rather than your personal benefit.

That last point trips people up constantly. Buying a $40 team polo shirt required by the nonprofit for a fundraising walk: deductible. Buying yourself a new pair of running shoes for that same walk because your old ones were worn out: not deductible, because you'd need shoes regardless of the volunteer work.

## The mileage deduction most volunteers never claim

Charitable mileage is one of the most underused deductions we see, especially among clients serving on nonprofit boards or driving for church, 4-H, or ag extension programs common around Hartford and Minnehaha County. The IRS sets a flat charitable mileage rate that's considerably lower than the business mileage rate, but it adds up. Someone driving 40 miles round trip to a food bank shift every other week for a year logs over 1,000 miles, and at the flat rate that's a deduction worth claiming.

You can also deduct actual out-of-pocket costs like gas and oil directly tied to the volunteer driving, instead of the flat rate, though you can't combine both methods for the same trip. Either way, parking fees and tolls paid while volunteering are deductible on top of whichever mileage method you choose.

## Costs that typically qualify

Here's how the common categories shake out for someone volunteering with a qualified 501(c)(3):

| Expense | Deductible? |
|---|---|
| Mileage driving to/from volunteer site | Yes, at the IRS charitable rate |
| Required uniforms not suitable for everyday wear | Yes |
| Supplies purchased and donated for the volunteer project | Yes |
| Travel costs for an out-of-town volunteer trip (lodging, meals) | Yes, if there's no significant personal recreation involved |
| Value of your time or professional services | No |
| Cost of childcare so you can volunteer | No |
| Personal meals while volunteering locally | No |

Out-of-town volunteer trips deserve their own mention because they're where the rules get strict fastest. If a board member travels to a national conference for the nonprofit and spends the trip in meetings and service work, lodging and travel are generally deductible. If that same trip includes three days of sightseeing tacked onto one afternoon of actual volunteer duty, the IRS is going to view that as a vacation with a charitable excuse, and the deduction won't survive scrutiny.

## Documentation the IRS actually wants to see

Donating your time doesn't require a receipt because there's no deduction to support. Donating your dollars is a different story, and the documentation bar rises with the amount.

For any single unreimbursed expense of $250 or more connected to volunteer work, you need written acknowledgment from the organization describing the services you provided and confirming you received nothing in return. For anything under that threshold, your own records, bank statements, receipts, and a mileage log with dates and destinations, are generally enough to support the deduction if you're ever asked about it.

A simple habit works better than trying to reconstruct a year of volunteer driving in April: keep a small notebook or a phone note with the date, organization, purpose, and miles for every trip. Clients who do this consistently tend to claim two or three times more in charitable mileage than clients who try to estimate it after the fact.

One more thing worth flagging for clients who volunteer through their own business, particularly in construction and agriculture where in-kind donations of equipment time or crew labor are common: donating the use of equipment or unpaid labor isn't deductible either, even when a business is the one giving it up. If a contractor lends a skid steer and an employee's time to clear a church parking lot, the business can deduct fuel and direct out-of-pocket costs tied to that job, but not the value of the equipment use or the wages that would have been paid. The [IRS Charities & Nonprofits Resource Hub](https://www.irs.gov/charities-non-profits) is a good reference for confirming an organization's qualified status before assuming any of this applies.

The underlying pattern is consistent: dollars out of pocket are fair game, hours and skill are not. Once clients understand that distinction, the recordkeeping becomes a lot less confusing, and the deduction stops getting left on the table.

## Common Questions

**Can I deduct the value of professional services I donate, like legal or accounting work?**
No. The IRS does not allow a deduction for the fair market value of your time or professional skill, even if you'd normally charge a client for that same work. Only your unreimbursed out-of-pocket costs tied to the volunteer service may qualify.

**What's the current charitable mileage rate, and how do I track it?**
The IRS sets a flat charitable mileage rate each year, separate from the business mileage rate, and it's detailed in [IRS Publication 526](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p526). Track it with a simple log noting the date, organization, purpose, and miles driven for each trip so the deduction holds up if questioned.

**Do I need a receipt for every volunteer expense I want to deduct?**
For any single expense of $250 or more, yes, you need written acknowledgment from the organization confirming your service and that you received nothing in return. Below that threshold, your own receipts and mileage logs are typically sufficient.

**Can my small business deduct donated equipment use or employee labor for a nonprofit project?**
No, the value of donated equipment use or labor isn't deductible, but direct out-of-pocket costs like fuel tied to that donated work generally are. This trips up construction and agriculture clients often, since equipment and crew time are their most common in-kind donations.
